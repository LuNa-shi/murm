Abstract
We prove a conjecture of Erdős: for every sufficiently large integerb, every rational number
a/b with 1 ≤a < bis a sum ofO(log logb) distinct positive unit fractions, with an absolute
implied constant. This order is best possible when the numerator varies. We also show that
both the number of expansions of1 with exactlyk distinct terms and the least integer at least2
that never occurs as a denominator in such an expansion grow doubly exponentially ink: their
double logarithms have orderk.

Nakayama’s study ofN(a,b) emphasized arithmetic criteria for expansions with few terms [18,
Section I]. The uniform problem asks how these minimum lengths behave as the numerator varies
over a fixed denominator. In 1950, Erdős reported de Bruijn’s boundN(b) ≪log b/log log logb and
improved it toN(b) ≪log b/log logb [6, p. 195, Theorem 1]. He proposed the double-logarithmic
upper bound and proved the matching lower order, already for the numeratorb−1 [6, p. 195,
Theorem 2]. The question appears again in Erdős and Graham [8, pp. 37–38] and is recorded
as Erdős Problem 304 [2]. Vose subsequently obtainedN(b) ≪√log b [23]; an explicit modern
statement of his uniform bound is given in [22, Lemma 2.2]. Theorem 1.1 proves Erdős’s conjecture
affirmatively. The lower bound is classical; the issue is the uniform upper bound.
A related line of work controls the denominators as well as the length. Tenenbaum and Yokota
proved that, for each fixedε >0 and every sufficiently largeb, everya/b ∈(0,1) has a distinct
expansion of length at most(1 + ε) log b/log logb, with all denominators at most4b(log b)2 log logb
[21, Theorem, p. 151]. Their length estimate serves this simultaneous constraint. Here we optimize
only the order of the length and leave the denominator sizes unrestricted.

The prescribed-denominator question originates in Erdős and Graham [8, p. 35]; see also
Problem 293 [1]. Van Doorn and Tang prove the lower boundv(k) ≥exp(ck2) for an absolute
c >0 [22, Theorem 1.1]. They also show that an exact prescribed denominator can be retained
while increasing the length of a distinct expansion [22, Lemma 2.1] and anticipate the connection
between uniform short expansions and a double-exponential lower bound forv(k) [22, Section 3].
We give the required marker-avoidance argument below, thereby deriving the qualitative order from
Theorem 1.1. A direct construction additionally gives the numerical lower slope in Corollary 1.3,
without quantifying the constantc2 in the uniform theorem. The elementary upper bound is
convenient rather than best known: sharper public upper bounds follow from the counting estimates
of Elsholtz and Planitzer [5, Corollary 3(2)].
The two consequences impose different requirements on a construction. To obtain many
expansions, it suffices to retain a denominator divisible by an integer with many prime factors. To
include a prescribed integerm in Dk, the exact term1/m must survive. The latter requirement
governs both the initial construction and the operation that increases its length.

The difficulty is to prevent the exceptional numerators from accumulating as these reductions

exceptional set. Hölder’s inequality then gives a recurrence for the exceptional proportions that
remains small throughout the descent. This combination of residue distribution, uniform divisor
moments, and the final passage from a dense family to every numerator is the main mechanism of
the proof.
The use of a common auxiliary denominator connects the proof to the classical divisor method.
Erdős’s construction writes suitable integers as sums of distinct divisors of a factorial and turns those
sums into unit fractions [6, Section 1, pp. 199–203]. Tenenbaum and Yokota obtain a shorter divisor
decomposition by choosing successive divisors close to the remaining numerator [21, Lemma 4 and
Section 3]. The present descent instead selects divisors through the residues they produce. Modular
control by finite Fourier methods also occurs in Croot’s work [3] and Martin’s development of it
[17, Section 4, Lemmas 11–13], where subset sums of modular inverses remove large prime-power
denominator factors. Here prime-product distributions supply many possible reductions, and a
uniform divisor moment keeps their exceptional sets under control across all levels. The moment
proof uses the prime-factor splitting method of Erdős [7, Section 3]; the truncation and shift
uniformity needed here are established below.
Section 2 isolates the dense-family statement and deduces Theorem 1.1 from it. Section 3 proves
the divisor estimate, Section 4 constructs the residue distributions, and Section 5 completes the
dense-family argument. The construction constants are fixed first, then a moment order large
enough for the descent, and finally a sufficiently large lower bound onS. Section 6 then deduces
Corollary 1.2. Theorem 1.1 supplies a short expansion containing a denominator with many prime
factors. Splitting that denominator over its divisors gives many distinct expansions, and an injective
padding operation gives every sufficiently large prescribed length. Divisor-rich denominators and
divisor-indexed splitting appear in Konyagin and Elsholtz [14, 4]; Konyagin explicitly records the
padding injection [14, p. 312].
For the prescribed-denominator consequence, Section 7 reserves1/m and uses a greedy prefix
that skips denominatorm. The remaining sum is smaller than every reserved reciprocal, so any
positive expansion of that remainder avoids all reserved denominators. Applying Theorem 1.1 gives
a short marked expansion, and a separate padding lemma retains the exact marker at every later
length. For an explicit length constant, Section 8 instead constructs the tail from a common supply
of rational numbers whose numerators divide one auxiliary integer. Prime-product residue collisions
and the quantitative three-prime theorem provide this supply. A divisor-density property of the
unreduced greedy denominator then groups the remaining numerator into few pieces. The resulting
length coefficient257/log 2 gives the lower slope after padding and absorbing the finitely many
small markers.
